Why this deck

The first version of our scorecard used weights of 35/25/20/10/10 chosen by judgment. We had no real default outcomes to test them against.

The Berka dataset is real bank data with real loan outcomes, so for the first time every weight can be measured.

Why this matters

A scorecard is only as honest as its target. Here the target is a real outcome the bank recorded, not a label we invented.

Status C and D loans are still running, so a few "good" C loans may yet default. That is a limit we flag at the end.

The loan's own repayments appear in trans as k_symbol = UVER. They are excluded, because a customer who is repaying obviously looks good.

Why a proxy for payment behavior?

Berka has one loan per account and no payment history before the loan, so "% on-time payments" cannot exist for a first-time borrower.

What the bank does see is whether the customer overdrew the account and was charged penalty interest (SANKC. UROK).

Where do the five criteria come from?

Not from the data. From the four questions every lender asks, plus one.

Have they repaid before?Payment Behavior
Can they afford it?Income & Capacity
Do they manage money responsibly?Transaction Behavior
How stable is the relationship?Account Relationship
What is the loan itself? (added)Demographics & Loan Context

The grouping is a design convention, not a law of nature. The data can tell us which groups help, which is what the next slides test.

Honest provenance

The four questions are in our project notes (risk_scoring.md). Our methodology calls the structure "FICO-style": group related metrics, weight the groups.

For comparison, FICO's public factors are payment history 35%, amounts owed 30%, length of history 15%, new credit 10%, credit mix 10%. Our original 35/25/20/10/10 echoes that shape but was an analyst's judgment, not copied and not fitted. The fifth criterion exists mainly to hold loan-size and age context.

Metric points = how well a bin separates good from bad

Example: min_balance in the 12 months before the loan

WoE = ln( good share ÷ bad share )
  • Positive bin: holds more good loans than average → safer.
  • Negative bin: holds more bad loans → riskier.
  • The points are measured, not guessed cutoffs like "≥95% → 100".

Reading the chart

Loans with a lowest balance of ≤ 500 default 26% of the time. Above 10,000 it is 0.7%. The bars show the log-ratio of those two shares.

A metric's Information Value (IV) adds up how far all its bins are from neutral: bigger IV = more predictive.

Filter out metrics that only look useful by luck

With only 76 bad loans, even a worthless metric scores IV ≈ 0.05 by chance.

We shuffle the labels 200 times to measure that chance level, per metric.

beats chance → keptwithin noise → droppedchance + 2 SD line

Dropped: age, gender, tenure, loan duration, district salary / unemployment …

The noise floor

Shuffling the good/bad labels destroys any real link, so whatever IV remains is pure chance. A metric must beat chance + 2 standard deviations to be kept.

Dropping age and gender this way is also the outcome we want for fairness.

Where each criteria weight comes from

1 · IV of each kept metric→2 · minus chance IV→3 · average per criteria→4 · share of total = weight
showing: data-derived

What changed

Payment conduct is #1, as assumed. Income collapsed from 25% to 5%: our income is only a proxy (money credited), so it is noisy.

Account relationship rose, mostly because accounts with a second holder (145 loans) had zero bad loans.

Can we drop or add a criterion? Test it.

Change in out-of-fold AUC vs the 5-criteria baseline (0.914)

A criterion earns its place if:

  • it is known before the decision
  • it beats chance on the noise test
  • removing it makes unseen loans score worse
  • or there is a policy reason (e.g. affordability)

How to read it

Each bar re-runs the whole pipeline (bins, noise filter, weights) with one criterion removed or added, on the same 100 train/test splits. "Better in x% of folds" counts how often the variant beat the baseline on the same split: near 50% means a coin flip.

Drop: Transaction Behavior is essential (worse in 99% of folds). Income and Demographics barely matter statistically.

Add: cash-flow stability helps slightly; standing orders add nothing. Standing orders also have no date, so some may postdate the loan: treat their high IV (1.7) with suspicion, not as a find.

score = offset + 50 ÷ ln 2 × ln( odds )
  • 600 pts = 20 : 1 odds (anchor).
  • +50 pts doubles the odds (PDO = 50).
  • So every score means a default probability. Drag the slider.
  • Grade bands kept: A ≥750 · B ≥680 · C ≥600 · D ≥500 · E.

Why not "300 + composite × 5.5"?

That old rule only stretched a 0–100 number to fit the range. It said nothing about risk.

The standard scorecard form ties points to odds, so "680" always means about 1.6% default. Calibration uses out-of-fold predictions so it is not overfit.

Drag: the two point scales move in lockstep. The % scale hugs the left edge for every good score.

  • Score = offset + factor × ln(odds). A different range just changes two numbers. Ranking and AUC are identical.
  • Why 300–850: credit people read it instantly, and our schema, grade bands and docs already use it.
  • Why not %: a probability squashes the safe end (0.2% vs 0.6% look equal but are 3× the risk) and cannot be added metric by metric.
  • Real cost: the range clips. 57 loans (8%) sit exactly on 300 or 850.

Three free choices

Any scorecard fixes (1) an anchor, here 600 points at 20:1 odds, (2) points to double the odds, here 50, and (3) a floor and ceiling. Nothing in the data forces 300 or 850.

0–1000 would be equally valid; we would simply re-cut the A–E bands. We keep 300–850 for continuity, and use the probability for what it is best at: pricing and provisioning. Score ranks, probability prices.

The 0–1000 rail here is a plain stretch of 300–850, to show the idea.

Does it work? Tested on loans it never saw

5-fold × 20 repeats. Bins, noise filter and weights are re-fitted on the training folds only. AUC: 0.5 = coin flip, 1.0 = perfect.

Be honest about the gap

Data-derived weights beat the judgment weights by about 0.02 AUC, which is less than one standard deviation across folds.

So the claim is "at least as good, and now justified", not "much better". The big win is the metrics and points, which is why even equal weights score 0.90.

Not leakage

The overdraft happened before the loan date, so the bank could see it at decision time.

But when a signal is 26 out of 26, it is better handled as a policy rule than blended into a smooth score where it dominates the weights.

What to remember

✓ What we can claim

  • Weights and points are measured on real outcomes, with no leakage.
  • Out-of-fold AUC 0.91; grades rank risk A → E.
  • Result holds when censored 1998 loans are removed (AUC 0.90).
  • Age, gender and district earn no weight.

⚠ What to caveat

  • Only 76 bad loans: weights are noisy, ±several points.
  • Income is a proxy; Payment Behavior is a proxy.
  • Weight gain over judgment is small (0.02 AUC).
  • Status C/D loans are still running; labels are not final.

Evidence replaced assumption. That is the whole point.
